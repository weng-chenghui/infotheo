## 1. Introduction

The original view of monodromy words in SMC-PGG is operational: <!-- REVIEW-FIX: [W2] Reserve L for languages; use ℓ for length -->
a word `w = σ_{i_1} σ_{i_2} ⋯ σ_{i_ℓ}` of length ℓ encodes a secret by permuting sheet IDs through the covering space. Parties execute the permutations; the endpoint determines the output.

A deeper observation: these words are themselves objects in **formal language theory** (FLT). The sets of words producing particular outcomes form languages; the algebraic structure of these languages (their syntactic monoids, variety membership, star-freeness, etc.) may connect to security properties of the protocol.

This note maps out the correspondence, honestly distinguishing three tiers:
- **Genuine**: mathematically proven connections
- **Plausible**: mathematically grounded conjectures, not yet proven
- **Suspicious**: analogies that may be forced or vacuous

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [W1] Add contribution statement -->
**Contributions.** We formally establish that: (1) fiber languages L_g are group languages in the Eilenberg variety sense (syntactic monoid divides G); (2) the Mazurkiewicz trace monoid framework applies directly to SMC-PGG under the independence-evaluation compatibility condition; and (3) the Cartier-Foata growth series identity gives trace enumeration for the full monoid. We propose as research directions: separability as a model for privacy, Krohn-Rhodes cascade protocols for extending beyond group languages, and variety-theoretic security classification. To our knowledge, this specific connection between Eilenberg varieties and MPC security has not been made explicit in the literature.

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [W5] Add standalone mathematical definitions for key concepts -->
**Key definitions** (standalone, independent of the Coq formalization):
- **Monodromy representation**: A group homomorphism ρ: π₁(X, x₀) → Sym(n) where X is a topological space with fundamental group π₁ and n is the number of sheets of a covering. In SMC-PGG, this is a finite group G ≤ Sym(n) with generators {ρ(σ₁), ..., ρ(σ_k)}.
- **Fiber language**: For g ∈ G, the set L_g = {w ∈ Σ\* : eval(w) = g}, where eval: Σ\* → G is the evaluation morphism extending ρ to words.
- **Independence relation**: A symmetric, irreflexive relation I ⊆ Σ × Σ specifying which generators commute. In SMC-PGG, (σ_i, σ_j) ∈ I iff ρ(σ_i) and ρ(σ_j) commute in G.
- **Trace equivalence**: The congruence ~_I on Σ\* generated by {ab ~ ba : (a,b) ∈ I}. Equivalence classes are called traces.
- **endpoint(w, s)**: The sheet reached by applying eval(w) to starting sheet s, i.e., ρ(w)(s).

## 2. The Four Decision Problems (The Four Decision Problems of Formal Language Theory; 形式言語の４つの決定問題)

Formal language theory centers on four fundamental decision problems for a language class **V**:

| Problem | Question | Formal |
|---------|----------|--------|
| **Membership** (所属判定) | Does language L belong to class V? | L ∈ V? |
| **Inclusion** (包含判定) | Is every V-language also in class W? | V ⊆ W? |
| **Separability** (分離可能性) | Given disjoint L₁, L₂, does a V-language S exist with L₁ ⊆ S and L₂ ∩ S = ∅? | ∃S ∈ V: L₁ ⊆ S, L₂ ⊆ S^c? |
<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S3] Clarify measurability definition -->
| **Measurability** (可測性) | Is L clopen (or more generally, Borel-measurable) in the pro-V topology on the profinite completion of Σ\*? | Is L V-measurable? |

**Decidability status** (for regular languages over finite words):

| Problem | Piecewise testable | Star-free | Group langs |
|---------|-------------------|-----------|-------------|
<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S1F] Eilenberg's theorem is structural, not algorithmic; decidability is elementary -->
| Membership | Decidable (Simon '75) | Decidable (Schützenberger '65) | Decidable (compute syntactic monoid, check group; see Pin) |
<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S2] Clarify Czerwiński citation -->
| Separability | Decidable (Czerwiński+ LICS '17 / journal '19) | Decidable (Place-Zeitoun '14) | Open / partial |
| Measurability | Decidable | Open | Open |

These are not idle questions — they correspond to different aspects of what we can algorithmically determine about the structure of a language.

## 4. Rigorous Assessment (Three Tiers)

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S4] Insert critical hypothesis I ⊆ ker(eval) -->
**Standing hypothesis.** Throughout, we assume the **independence-evaluation compatibility condition**: the independence relation I on generators is compatible with the evaluation morphism eval: Σ\* → G. That is, if (a, b) ∈ I (generators a and b are independent/commuting in the trace monoid), then eval(a) and eval(b) commute in G. In SMC-PGG, this holds by construction: the RAAG relations defining the trace monoid are exactly the commutation relations respected by the monodromy representation ρ. Without this hypothesis, trace-equivalent words could map to different group elements, and the fiber-language/trace-language correspondence would break down.

### Tier 1: Genuinely True

These are mathematical facts, not analogies.

**Trace monoid = FLT object.** The Mazurkiewicz trace monoid M(Σ, I) is a standard object of study in formal language theory and concurrency theory. Our `trace_equiv` in `pgg_raag.v` is literally Mazurkiewicz equivalence. The Cartier-Foata normal form we proved is the standard representative selection for traces. This is not an analogy — it's the same mathematical object used for the same purpose.

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S5] Diekert-Gastin conditional on independence-evaluation compatibility -->
**Diekert-Gastin theorem applies.** The characterization of first-order definable trace languages (Diekert-Gastin 2006) applies directly to our trace monoid. Languages definable in FO[<] over traces correspond to aperiodic elements of the trace monoid — this is the trace-monoid generalization of Schützenberger's theorem. Given the standing hypothesis (I ⊆ ker(eval)), each fiber L_g is a union of trace classes, so it is a well-defined trace language. The Diekert-Gastin characterization therefore applies to our fiber languages.

**RAAG word problem complexity.** The word problem for right-angled Artin groups is solvable in linear time (via trace reduction). This is a known result in combinatorial group theory / formal language theory. Our `trace_equiv_dec` is an instance.

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S6] Disambiguate G (variety) vs G (specific group); clarify syntactic monoid argument -->
**Eilenberg variety theory on fibers.** Each fiber language L_g = {w ∈ Σ\* : eval(w) = g} is a regular language (recognized by the finite group G acting as a transformation monoid). Its syntactic monoid divides G. Since any monoid dividing a finite group is itself a group (submonoids of groups are subgroups; quotients of groups are groups), the syntactic monoid of L_g is a finite group. By Eilenberg's variety theorem, L_g therefore belongs to the variety **Gp** of group languages (the variety of languages whose syntactic monoid is a finite group). This is a theorem, not speculation.

*Notation*: We write **Gp** for the Eilenberg variety of group languages and *G* for the specific monodromy group of the protocol.

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [F1-related] Align Tier 1 claim with corrected §6 statement -->
**Trace monoid growth series = Clique polynomial identity.** The growth series of the full trace monoid M(Σ, I) is determined by the clique polynomial of the commutation graph (Cartier-Foata / Viennot identity). This is what our `n_traces` / `search_space` framework builds on. The total number of traces of a given length follows from this identity, connecting FLT (trace counting) to combinatorics (graph polynomials) to security (entropy). Note: computing trace counts within a *specific fiber* L_g requires additional work beyond the clique polynomial identity.

### Tier 2: Plausible but Unproven

These are mathematically grounded conjectures that haven't been worked out.

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S7] Distinguish language-theoretic separability from crypto adversary model -->
**Separability as privacy.** The separability problem asks: given disjoint languages L_g and L_h (fibers for g ≠ h), can they be separated by a language in some restricted class V? If V represents "what a t-coalition can observe," then V-separability of L_g and L_h means the coalition can distinguish the secret g from h. V-inseparability means privacy. This is a clean conceptual mapping — but two significant gaps remain: (1) making "what a t-coalition can observe" into a precise language class V requires concrete construction that hasn't been done; and (2) language-theoretic separability is a deterministic, non-uniform word classification, whereas cryptographic privacy involves probabilistic, adaptive adversaries with computational bounds. Bridging these two models requires a formal reduction showing that any efficient distinguisher corresponds to a test in some language class V. This reduction does not yet exist.

**Variety hierarchy as finer security.** Different levels of the variety lattice (piecewise testable ⊂ star-free ⊂ group languages ⊂ regular) could give a finer security classification than the binary "secure/insecure." A protocol might be star-free-secure but not piecewise-testable-secure. This would mean the adversary can distinguish secrets using star-free tests but not piecewise testable ones. Plausible and interesting, but no one has developed this.

**FO-definability as adversary power.** If the adversary's view corresponds to an FO-definable trace language, then Diekert-Gastin gives us decidable tools. The adversary's partial observation (seeing some generators but not others) naturally restricts to a sub-alphabet projection, which preserves FO-definability. This chain of reasoning is sound, but the first step (adversary view = FO-definable) is assumed, not proven.

### Tier 3: Suspicious / Weak

These analogies may be forced or vacuous.

**Measurability → security.** The profinite topology on the free monoid induces a notion of "measurability" for languages. Claiming this connects to "quantitative security" is currently hand-waving. The profinite topology is about asymptotic structure (limits of finite quotients), while security is about concrete finite instances. The arrow `←─???→` in the diagram is honest: there's no concrete construction here.

**CFL relevance.** Context-free languages don't obviously arise in SMC-PGG. The word problem for RAAGs is decidable in linear time (it's essentially a regular/trace language problem). Pushing to CFLs would require stack-like structure that covering spaces don't naturally provide. Mentioning CFLs risks scope creep.

**"Choosing a word = computing."** The phrase is imprecise. Choosing a word from a language is sampling, not computing. The party that executes the word is computing (state transformation). The language-theoretic properties apply to the *set of possible words*, not to the act of choosing. Be precise: the computation is the automaton execution; the language theory applies to classifying the outcome sets.

## 5. Key Clarification: Is Party Computation "Real" FLT Computation?

Yes, but as **automaton-as-transducer**, not automaton-as-recognizer.

Each party IS a **permutation automaton**: a DFA where every input letter induces a permutation on the state set.

| FLT concept | SMC-PGG realization |
|-------------|---------------------|
| State set Q | Sheet IDs {1, ..., n} |
| Alphabet Σ | Generator indices {σ₁, ..., σ_k} |
| Transition δ(q, σᵢ) | ρ(σᵢ)(q) — monodromy action |
<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S9] Transformation monoid = G assumes generators span G -->
| Transformation monoid | ⟨ρ(σ₁), ..., ρ(σ_k)⟩ ≤ G (= G when generators span the monodromy group) |
| Initial state | Starting sheet s |
| "Accepting" state | Target sheet t |

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S10] Elementary fact about permutation automata, not Eilenberg's theorem -->
The language L_{s→t} = {w ∈ Σ\* : endpoint(w, s) = t} is a **regular group language**. Its syntactic monoid divides the transformation group of the automaton, which is a subgroup of Sym(n). Since any monoid dividing a finite group is itself a group, the syntactic monoid of L_{s→t} is a finite group. (This is an elementary observation in algebraic automata theory; see Pin, *Mathematical Foundations of Automata Theory*, Ch. 4.)

So the computation is genuine:
- The party is a finite-state machine
- The transformation monoid is the monodromy group
- The language of words reaching a particular outcome is a group language

But the party's *purpose* is state transformation (transducing input sheet to output sheet), not language recognition (accept/reject). The formal language theory applies to the **sets of words** that produce particular outcomes, not to the party's operational role.

This distinction matters: saying "the protocol recognizes a group language" is technically correct (you can read off acceptance from the endpoint) but misleading about the protocol's purpose.

## 6. Direction 1: How SMC-PGG May Help Formal Language Theory

SMC-PGG provides formal language theory with something it often lacks: **concrete, motivated, finite instances** where the abstract machinery has operational consequences.

### Concrete trace monoid instances with security motivation

Most FLT work on trace monoids is either asymptotic (complexity classes) or abstract (variety theory). SMC-PGG gives small, explicit trace monoids where:
- The independence graph has combinatorial meaning (which generators commute)
- Trace counts have operational meaning (entropy / security)
- The fibers L_g are explicit, enumerable regular languages

This could serve as a testing ground for conjectures in trace theory.

### Clique polynomial as trace-counting tool

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [F1] Correct clique polynomial claim — applies to full trace monoid, not sublanguages -->
The growth series of the full trace monoid M(Σ, I) satisfies 1/G(t) = Σ_C (-1)^|C| t^|C| summed over cliques C of the *commutation* graph (complement of independence graph). This is the classical Cartier-Foata / Viennot identity connecting trace enumeration to graph combinatorics. In SMC-PGG, the total number of trace classes of length ℓ follows from this identity. However, counting traces within a *specific fiber* L_g requires additional structure (e.g., transfer-matrix methods on the trace monoid automaton) and is not polynomial-time in general. The security interpretation (each trace class = one distinguishable execution pattern) gives the growth series new meaning.

### ℓ-freeness as a new property

The property that "no non-trivial relation holds among generators for words of length ≤ ℓ" (what we call ℓ-freeness in the RAAG) connects the word problem to trace equivalence in a length-bounded way. <!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S13] Qualify novelty claim; cite related work -->
This is a natural FLT property (bounded-length freeness in a quotient monoid) that, to our knowledge, has not been studied in the protocol context. Related concepts exist in geometric group theory (cogrowth series, small cancellation conditions, Dehn functions), but the specific connection to trace equivalence and security bounds appears new.

### A computational use case for group languages

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S14] Demote to interpretation; [W10] Replace circular claim with specific statement -->
The protocol can be *interpreted* as a distributed group language recognizer: each party's local computation corresponds to a permutation automaton transition, and reconstruction determines which fiber L_g the input word belongs to. This interpretation gives Eilenberg's variety theory a concrete application domain. Specifically, the fiber construction provides a new family of trace languages arising from cryptographic protocols, giving FLT concrete finite instances to study.

## 7. Direction 2: How Formal Language Theory May Help SMC-PGG

### Variety-theoretic classification → finer security

If fiber languages L_g belong to specific sub-varieties of group languages, this classification could yield finer security guarantees than "the group is non-abelian."

For example: if L_g is star-free (aperiodic syntactic monoid), the adversary's distinguishing power is limited to FO-definable properties. If L_g is piecewise testable, even less. The variety lattice could give a hierarchy of security levels, each decidable (given the syntactic monoid).

**Status**: Plausible. The fiber L_g has syntactic monoid dividing G, so it's always a group language. But sub-variety membership within group languages (which subgroup? which structure?) could still discriminate.

### Separability decidability → algorithmic privacy verification

If "adversary can distinguish g from h" corresponds to V-separability of L_g and L_h for some language class V, then decidability of V-separability gives an *algorithm* for verifying privacy. For piecewise testable separability, this is decidable (Czerwiński et al. 2019). For star-free separability, also decidable (Place-Zeitoun 2014).

**Status**: Plausible but requires the concrete construction of "adversary observation class V."

### Star-free / FO-definability → logical adversary characterization

Diekert-Gastin's theorem characterizes FO-definable trace languages. If the adversary's view is FO-definable, we get a logical characterization of what they can and cannot deduce. This connects to descriptive complexity: the adversary's power is bounded by first-order logic over the trace partial order.

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [F3/S8F] FO-definability NOT preserved under arbitrary morphisms; only inverse morphisms -->
**Status**: Plausible. The projection from full trace to adversary's sub-alphabet view preserves regularity (regularity is closed under morphisms). However, FO-definability (= star-freeness = aperiodicity) is preserved under *inverse* morphisms but NOT under arbitrary forward morphisms or letter-erasing projections. The operative direction for the adversary model is: the adversary's observation is the *preimage* of their observable sub-alphabet projection, which does preserve FO-definability. This distinction requires careful formalization.

## 7.2. Direction 2 Extended: SMC-PGG Variants for FLT Computation

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [S15] Distinguish computing φ(w) from recognizing L_g -->
A deeper observation: SMC-PGG computes the group element g = eval(w) for an input word w. This is equivalent to determining which fiber language L_g = eval⁻¹(g) the word belongs to — i.e., it implicitly recognizes group languages (though the protocol's purpose is computing a group element, not accept/reject classification). Can we extend this?

### Krohn-Rhodes protocol decomposition

The **Krohn-Rhodes theorem** decomposes any finite automaton into a cascade (wreath product) of:
- **Simple group components** — each recognized by a group language
- **Aperiodic (counter-free) components** — each recognized by a star-free language

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [F4] "counter operations" contradicts "counter-free"; [S16] K-R uses simple groups; [S17] cascade composability non-trivial -->
SMC-PGG handles group layers via monodromy-based sub-protocols (though note: K-R decomposes into *simple* group components, so the monodromy group G may need to be further decomposed along its composition series). A new **"aperiodic SMC" protocol** could handle counter-free layers. These aperiodic components are characterized by the absence of non-trivial groups in the syntactic monoid — they require only reset-type operations, not group structure. Cascading the two could yield **secure recognition of all regular languages**, though this requires a non-trivial composability argument: the wreath product wiring between layers must preserve security, and the cascade may involve alternating group and aperiodic layers.

**Status**: Mathematically grounded conjecture. The K-R decomposition is a theorem; the protocol cascade is a construction to be designed. This is the most concrete direction here.

### Secure language recognition as MPC task

Frame the problem: N parties jointly hold a secret word w (each party knows some letters). They want to determine if w ∈ L without revealing w. For group languages, SMC-PGG solves this. The K-R cascade extends to all regular languages. This is a natural MPC problem that connects to existing secure computation literature.

**Status**: Plausible. The MPC framing is standard; the K-R cascade construction is the novel part.

### Dot-depth hierarchy → protocol complexity hierarchy

The Straubing-Thérien hierarchy (dot-depth) stratifies star-free languages:

```
Σ₀ ⊂ Σ₁ ⊂ Σ₂ ⊂ ⋯ ⊂ star-free ⊂ regular
```

<!-- REVIEW-FIX: [F5] Level 0 = {∅, Σ*}, not Boolean combinations of A*aA*; [S18F] dot-depth ≠ round complexity -->
Each level could conceivably correspond to a different number of cascaded protocol rounds. For reference: Level 0 consists of only {∅, Σ\*} (trivial languages); Level 1/2 (Σ₁) consists of finite unions of languages A\*a₁A\*a₂...A\*aₖA\*; Level 1 consists of Boolean combinations of Σ₁ languages.

**Status**: Speculative, and possibly vacuous. Dot-depth measures language-theoretic structural complexity (quantifier alternation in FO[<]), while MPC round complexity depends on protocol design and computational model. There is no established or obvious correspondence between these notions — a language of high dot-depth could potentially be recognized in constant rounds if the group has the right structure. This is an open question, not a conjectured equality.

### Beyond regular

Composing protocol rounds with stack-like covering space topology could potentially reach context-free recognition. A covering space with infinite fiber (universal cover of a wedge of circles) gives a tree — i.e., a pushdown structure.

**Status**: Highly speculative. Mentioning for completeness; don't build on this.
